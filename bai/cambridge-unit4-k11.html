Southeast Asia's Moment

ASEAN was found in 1967 by five countries that had little in common except a wish for stability. Few observers at the time would have predicted what followed. The bloc survived the Asian financial crisis of the late 1990s, welcomed new members, and grew into an economy that, taken together, would rank among five largest in the world. Although the crisis years were painful, but the region recovered, and across Southeast Asia living standards have risen steadily for three decades, with hundreds of millions of people escaping poverty.

Integration has given the region something what earlier generations could hardly have imagined: factories in Viet Nam selling to 680 million consumers, and banks in Singapore financing projects in every member state. As supply chains have spread across borders, living standards in the poorer member states have raised faster than at any time in the past. No sooner had the leaders signed the new trade deal when several governments demanded changes to protect their own industries. In practice, the habit of deciding everything by consensus often prevents it to respond quickly when a crisis comes.

ASEAN's leaders speak often of a shared commitment to promoting trade, investment and cooperating across borders. Several members have moved quickly open their economies to foreign competition. The gap between the richest and the poorest member states remains wide, however, and some governments still protect local industries behind high barriers. Unless the bloc will find a way to turn its promises into action, the next decade may test its unity more severely than any before.

The Mekong: One River, Many Masters

Few rivers matter to more people than the Mekong. Rising in the mountains of Tibet, it flows more than 4,000 kilometres through six countries before reaching the sea in southern Viet Nam. Along the way it waters the rice fields of Thailand and Cambodia, fills the nets of Tonle Sap, the largest lake in Southeast Asia, and supports one of the biggest inland fisheries on Earth. For the more than 60 million people who live along its banks, the Mekong is not scenery; it is a lifeline.

That lifeline is now being reshaped by concrete. In the past three decades, China has built a series of large hydropower dams on the upper Mekong, which it calls the Lancang, and Laos has followed with dams of its own on the mainstream, hoping to become 'the battery of Southeast Asia'. Dozens more are planned. The dams generate valuable electricity and can store water in the dry season, but they also change the river's natural rhythm, trapping sediment and blocking the migration of fish.

The effects are felt most sharply at the end of the river, in Viet Nam's Mekong Delta. In a natural year, the river carries millions of tonnes of sediment downstream, where it settles on fields as a natural fertiliser and slowly builds new land that holds back the sea. When dams trap that sediment, the delta begins to starve. At the same time, changing water flows have been linked to the severe droughts and salt water invasions that damaged hundreds of thousands of hectares of rice in recent years. Fish catches, too, have fallen in many areas.

Managing a river shared by six countries was never going to be simple. The Mekong River Commission, set up in 1995 by Cambodia, Laos, Thailand and Viet Nam, studies the river and advises its members, but it has no power to order any government to change its plans - and China, which controls the headwaters, is only a dialogue partner, not a full member. Beijing has instead promoted its own forum, the Lancang-Mekong Cooperation, through which it funds projects and, in recent years, has begun to share more water data.

The Mekong has therefore become a test case for the region: can countries that compete for the same water also cooperate to protect it? There are small signs of progress, from better data sharing to joint studies of the river's health. Yet the fundamental problem remains - every dam built upstream is a decision taken by one country whose costs are paid by others downstream. Whether Southeast Asia can solve that problem together will say much about its future.

ASEAN at a Crossroads

A. Judged by its first half-century, ASEAN is one of Asia's great success stories: a group of poor, divided countries that turned itself into one of the world's largest economies and kept the peace among its members. Yet the decade ahead may test the bloc more severely than any before. Old challenges are becoming sharper, new ones are appearing, and ASEAN's famous habit of moving slowly and deciding everything by consensus is being questioned as never before.

B. The ASEAN Economic Community, launched in 2015, was supposed to create a single market, but much of the project remains unfinished. Tariffs have largely gone, yet businesses still complain about non-tariff barriers, differing regulations and restrictions that keep services such as banking, health and education mostly national. A company in Jakarta or Ha Noi that wants to operate across the region still faces eleven sets of rules. Until those hidden barriers fall, the 'single market' will remain more of a slogan than a reality.

C. The pandemic of the early 2020s taught ASEAN a hard lesson about health. When COVID-19 spread across the region, each country responded alone: borders closed at different times, rules differed from one capital to the next, and poorer members struggled to buy vaccines. Since then, the bloc has promised to prepare together, with shared stockpiles of medicine, joint emergency teams and a regional centre for public health emergencies. Progress, however, is slow, and health spending in several member states remains low.

D. Geography has placed Southeast Asia directly in harm's way. The region's great river deltas, where tens of millions live and farm, are among the lowest and flattest on Earth, and sea levels are rising around them. Typhoons are growing stronger, floods and heatwaves more frequent, and cities from Jakarta to Ho Chi Minh City face worsening inundation. Adapting will cost enormous sums that poorer members do not have, making climate change not only an environmental threat but a test of regional solidarity.

E. In the digital economy, ASEAN contains both the future and the past. Singapore ranks among the world's most connected societies, with near-universal high-speed internet and a thriving technology sector. Elsewhere in the bloc, however, millions still lack reliable connections, digital skills are scarce, and governments struggle to protect citizens from online crime. If the gap keeps widening, the region's growth will become unbalanced: a few digital hubs racing ahead while rural communities are left further behind.

F. The region is also splitting in two demographically. Thailand and Singapore are ageing rapidly, with shrinking workforces and rising pension costs, and Viet Nam is not far behind them. At the other extreme, the Philippines and Indonesia still have large, young populations entering the job market every year. Managed well, these two halves could complement each other, with workers moving to where they are needed. Managed badly, they will produce unemployment in one half and labour shortages in the other.

G. Finally, ASEAN must change how it powers itself. Demand for electricity in the region is among the fastest-growing in the world, and most of that power still comes from coal and gas. Solar and wind resources are plentiful, especially in Viet Nam and the Philippines, but power grids are old, investment is expensive, and each country plans its energy future separately. A long-discussed regional power grid, linking all eleven states, could let them share clean electricity - if they can agree on how to build and pay for it.
