The Green Lungs of the City

A Modern cities are becoming uncomfortably hot. In many large urban areas, temperatures are now regularly several degrees higher than in the surrounding countryside, a phenomenon known as the urban heat island effect. The main cause is the materials from which cities are built. Dark surfaces such as asphalt roads and concrete pavements absorb heat from the sun during the day and release it only slowly at night, so the air above them stays warm long after sunset. Measurements in some metropolitan areas have recorded differences of up to seven degrees Celsius between the city centre and nearby rural districts. The consequences are serious: heatwaves become more dangerous, particularly for the elderly, and demand for air conditioning rises sharply. As urban populations continue to grow, city planners are searching urgently for ways to cool their streets, and one of the most promising answers has been growing above them all along: trees.

B Trees lower temperatures in two main ways. The first is simple shade. A surface exposed to direct summer sun can be as much as 25 degrees Celsius hotter than a similar surface sheltered beneath a tree canopy, and anyone who has crossed a city square on a hot afternoon will know how dramatic the difference feels. The second process, known as evapotranspiration, is less obvious but even more powerful. Tree roots draw water up from the soil, and the leaves then release much of it into the air as vapour. This cools the surrounding air in much the same way that sweat cools human skin. A single mature tree can release as much as 400 litres of water on a hot day, and the air directly beneath its canopy is often two to four degrees cooler than the air over a nearby open street. Indeed, several research studies suggest that evapotranspiration accounts for the majority of a tree’s cooling effect on the air around it, with shade playing a smaller, though still significant, role.

C The advantages of urban trees extend well beyond temperature control. Their leaves trap dust and absorb a range of air pollutants, including nitrogen dioxide and fine particles from vehicle exhausts, which are linked to asthma and heart disease. One frequently cited study estimated that the trees of a single large American city remove several hundred tonnes of polluted material from the air each year. Green surroundings also appear to benefit mental health: surveys consistently link easy access to green space with lower levels of stress and faster recovery from mental fatigue. There are financial gains, too. Homes on tree-lined streets are highly sought after, and properties in leafy neighbourhoods can sell for up to 15 per cent more than comparable houses on bare streets. Trees also intercept heavy rainfall, reducing the pressure on drainage systems and lowering the risk of flooding after storms.

D Not everyone is convinced, however, that large-scale tree planting is money well spent. Critics point out that planting a single street tree can cost a city council $800 or more once the pavement has been cut and protective guards installed, and that pruning and watering add further expense year after year. Roots can crack pavements, fallen leaves block drains, and in dry regions the irrigation that young trees need may compete with supplies of drinking water. Some urban designers argue that reflective roof coatings and artificial shade structures can cool buildings more cheaply and predictably. Supporters of planting respond that such comparisons ignore everything trees provide besides shade, from wildlife habitats to cleaner air. They also cite research suggesting that every dollar invested in urban trees returns several dollars in combined benefits. Both sides agree on one point: trees planted without a proper maintenance budget often die within a few years, wasting the original investment entirely.

E Despite the debate, a growing number of cities are putting trees at the centre of their climate plans, and the results are beginning to be measured. In Medellín, Colombia, the city created a network of ‘green corridors’ along busy roads and waterways, and local temperatures in some districts subsequently fell by around two degrees Celsius. Singapore has spent decades pursuing its vision of a ‘city in a garden’, integrating planting into new buildings as well as streets. Melbourne, in Australia, has gone further still by setting a precise long-term target: the city plans to raise its tree canopy from 22 per cent of its total area to 40 per cent by 2040. Planners there have also noted that the poorest districts, where residents suffer most in heatwaves, often have the fewest trees, so new planting is being directed there first.

F Few specialists claim that trees alone can solve the problem of overheating cities. Most argue instead that planting programmes must be combined with other measures, such as pale building materials that reflect sunlight, green roofs, and reduced car traffic in central areas. The choice of species matters as well: drought-resistant native trees are far more likely to survive in a changing climate than thirsty imported varieties, and involving local residents in planting and care greatly improves a young tree’s chances. Nevertheless, the overall picture is clear. Trees are among the few urban investments that cool the air, clean it, and become more valuable with every passing decade. Cities that plant them carefully today are, in effect, installing natural air conditioning for the generations that follow.

Farming Upwards

A By 2050, almost 70 per cent of the world’s population is expected to live in cities, yet nearly all of their food will still be produced in the countryside and transported over long distances. This growing separation between where people live and where their food is grown has encouraged interest in vertical farming: the practice of cultivating crops in stacked layers inside climate-controlled buildings, from converted warehouses to purpose-built towers. Because plants are grown close to the consumers who eat them, supporters argue, vertical farming could shorten supply chains, reduce waste, and make urban food supplies more secure. What began as a speculative idea is now attracting substantial commercial investment, although whether it can ever operate profitably at scale remains an open question.

B The concept itself is far from new. In the 1930s, the American plant scientist William Gericke demonstrated that crops could be grown without soil in nutrient-rich water, a method he called hydroponics. Half a century later, in the 1980s, Japanese researchers built the first commercial ‘plant factories’, in which vegetables were produced under artificial light in sealed rooms, though the high price of electricity at the time limited their commercial success. The modern version of the idea took shape in 1999, when Dickson Despommier, a professor of public health at Columbia University, challenged his students to design a farm capable of feeding the entire population of Manhattan. The class calculated that gardens on every available rooftop in the borough could provide only a fraction of the food its residents needed, so Despommier concluded that the farm would have to move indoors and grow upwards instead.

C In a typical vertical farm, plants are arranged on shelving units under rows of LED lights, which can be adjusted to provide the precise colour spectrum each crop requires at each stage of its growth. Most facilities abandon soil altogether. In hydroponic systems, the roots sit in shallow channels of water enriched with dissolved minerals. In aeroponic systems, by contrast, the roots hang freely in the air and are sprayed at intervals with a fine nutrient mist, a technique that supporters claim promotes faster growth. Meanwhile, sensors continuously track temperature, humidity and carbon dioxide levels, and software adjusts conditions automatically, so that every plant receives exactly what it needs, whenever it needs it.

D The potential advantages are considerable. Because layers are stacked vertically, a single hectare of vertical farm can, by some estimates, match the output of ten or more hectares of conventional farmland. Water use is equally striking: since water that is not absorbed by the plants is captured and recirculated rather than lost to the soil, indoor farms typically consume up to 95 per cent less water than field agriculture. Crops can be harvested throughout the year, regardless of droughts, floods or seasons, and because the growing environment is sealed, pests are largely excluded, so pesticides are rarely needed. Locating farms within cities also cuts ‘food miles’ dramatically, meaning produce reaches shops within hours of being picked and stays fresh for longer.

E Set against these benefits, however, are formidable economic obstacles, above all the cost of electricity. Artificial lighting is by far the largest operating expense, and even with the most efficient LEDs, producing ‘sunlight’ indoors remains expensive. This fundamental problem explains why commercial vertical farms grow almost exclusively quick, high-value crops such as leafy greens, herbs and strawberries. Staple crops are a different matter: researchers have estimated that a loaf of bread made from wheat grown in a vertical farm would cost several times more than a conventionally produced loaf, because the energy required vastly exceeds the crop’s market value. Until energy becomes much cheaper, the technology seems poorly suited to feeding the world its basic calories.

F The industry’s recent history offers a further warning. During the 2010s, investors poured billions of dollars into vertical-farming start-ups, attracted by optimistic projections of rapid growth. Yet a series of high-profile failures followed. AeroFarms, once among the sector’s largest and most celebrated companies, filed for bankruptcy protection in 2023, while the French company Agricool had been placed into receivership a year earlier. Analysts attribute the collapses partly to rising energy prices, which squeezed margins that were already thin, and partly to overexpansion: several firms built enormous facilities before they had secured reliable buyers for their produce. The failures have made investors noticeably more cautious about funding new ventures.

G Despite such setbacks, few specialists expect vertical farming to disappear. LED technology continues to become cheaper and more efficient, and farms increasingly pair their operations with renewable energy, which reduces both costs and carbon emissions. The strongest growth is predicted in places where conventional agriculture is hardest: arid regions short of water, and densely populated city-states such as Singapore, which currently imports more than 90 per cent of its food and has set targets to produce far more of it locally by 2030. The emerging consensus is that vertical farming will supplement rather than replace traditional agriculture, taking over a share of the market for salad crops and soft fruit while fields continue to supply the world’s grains.

The Riddle of Dreams

A For roughly six years of an average lifetime, we are not awake at all - we are dreaming. Each night, the sleeping brain produces vivid stories, populated by familiar people and impossible places, which it then promptly forgets. For most of human history, dreams were treated as messages: warnings from gods, visits from ancestors, or glimpses of the future. Modern science has been slower to take dreams seriously, partly because they are so difficult to observe - a dream can only be studied through the imperfect memory of the dreamer. Yet researchers have begun to ask a deceptively simple question: why does the brain devote so much of the night to an activity that appears to serve no purpose at all?

B The modern study of dreaming began in 1953, when researchers in Chicago noticed that sleeping volunteers periodically moved their eyes rapidly beneath their closed lids. Volunteers woken during these periods of rapid eye movement - REM sleep - usually reported dreams, and most dreaming is concentrated in this stage. Brain scans have since revealed the pattern of REM sleep: areas responsible for vision and emotion become highly active, while the prefrontal cortex, the region associated with logic and self-control, falls unusually quiet. This pattern explains the peculiar character of dreams: impossible events accepted without surprise, emotions running unchecked. It also casts doubt on claims that dreaming is merely a side effect of the brain shutting down. Far from resting, the dreaming brain consumes almost as much energy as the waking one.

C One influential answer comes from Dr Mara Ellison, a neuroscientist who argues that dreaming is an essential part of how the brain handles memory. During sleep, she proposes, the brain replays experiences of the day, strengthening connections that matter and discarding those that do not; dreams are what this sorting feels like from the inside. In one widely cited experiment, volunteers who napped after learning to navigate a virtual maze improved far more if their dreams touched on the maze than if they did not. Crucially, however, the dream reports in Ellison’s studies rarely reproduce an experience exactly as it happened. Instead, they combine fragments of recent events with older memories in bizarre arrangements. For Ellison, this shows the brain is not simply replaying the day but reorganising it, breaking experiences down so their useful elements can be stored alongside related knowledge.

D A different explanation comes from the Finnish psychologist Jussi Korhonen, who views dreaming primarily as a form of rehearsal. According to his threat simulation theory, dreams evolved because they allowed our ancestors to practise perceiving dangers and escaping from them, in an environment where mistakes carried no cost. The content of dreams themselves supports the theory: across cultures, being chased is by far the most common dream theme, and dangerous animals and hostile strangers appear far more often than in waking life. Children in regions affected by war or violence, whose daily lives contain genuine threats, report significantly more threatening dreams than children in safer places. For Korhonen, the dreaming brain is running a training programme written by evolution.

E Dr Elena Vasquez, a psychiatrist, focuses instead on what dreaming does to emotion. She describes REM sleep as ‘overnight therapy’: during this stage, the brain reactivates emotional memories in an environment from which the stress hormone noradrenaline is almost entirely absent. Replayed without its usual chemical charge, she argues, the memory can be processed while its emotional sting is gradually removed. Over time, the event itself is retained, but the distress attached to it fades. Support comes indirectly from patients with post-traumatic stress disorder, whose REM sleep is frequently disrupted. Such patients are plagued by recurring nightmares in which the traumatic event returns at full emotional force - precisely what Vasquez’s model predicts if the normal processing system breaks down.

F Not all researchers accept that dreams have a function at all. The psychiatrist Allan Hobson famously proposed that dreams are essentially meaningless: during REM sleep, the brainstem sends random signals to the higher brain, which then does its best to weave them into a story. On this view, dreams are a by-product of brain housekeeping, not a tool shaped by evolution. Functional theories also face an awkward fact: some people, including patients with certain brain lesions, appear to stop dreaming altogether, yet show few obvious deficits in memory or emotional health. Others dream but never remember doing so, and function perfectly well. Dreams are also reported from non-REM sleep, although they tend to be shorter and less vivid, which complicates any account tying dreaming too closely to one sleep stage.

G Whatever their purpose, dreams are becoming easier to study. In a Japanese study, researchers used brain scanners to predict the broad categories of what volunteers were dreaming about - whether, for example, a person or a landscape - with an accuracy of around 70 per cent. Even more striking are experiments on lucid dreaming, where sleepers become aware they are dreaming while remaining asleep. In 2021, four laboratories independently demonstrated two-way communication with lucid dreamers, who answered simple questions - including basic arithmetic - by moving their eyes in agreed patterns. Such techniques promise to replace the dreamer’s unreliable memory with direct observation. They may also let researchers test functional theories properly for the first time, asking dreamers what they are experiencing as it happens rather than hours later.

H Where does this leave the question of why we dream? No single theory currently explains the full range of evidence, and each faces findings it cannot accommodate. It may be that the search for one purpose is itself mistaken: an activity as old and as widespread as dreaming could plausibly serve several functions at once, or different functions in different species. What is no longer seriously in doubt is that dreaming is not an idle accident of sleep. The dreaming brain is working - sorting, rehearsing, soothing, perhaps all of these - and the task for science is to determine what that work achieves.
